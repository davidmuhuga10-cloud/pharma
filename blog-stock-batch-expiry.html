How to track pharmacy stock by batch and expiry date

Published 9 November 2026 · Pharma guides

Every pack of medicine you stock has two things that matter beyond its name: which batch it came from, and when it expires. A pharmacy that tracks both, rather than just a total count per drug, sells the oldest stock first and rarely gets caught with expired stock on the shelf.

Why batch tracking beats a simple count

If you only track "how many packs of Drug X do we have," you can't tell which ones are closest to expiry. Two batches of the same drug, bought weeks apart, can have very different expiry dates. Keeping stock by batch means the system — not memory — knows which pack to sell next.

In Pharma, every time you receive stock you record its batch and expiry date, and the oldest-expiring batch is what gets sold first automatically. You don't have to remember which shelf has the older stock.

Set an expiry-warning window that's actually useful

A warning that fires the day before expiry is too late to do anything about. A warning that fires a year early just becomes noise you ignore. Pick a window based on how fast a drug actually moves: fast-selling items can have a shorter warning window, slow movers need a longer one so you have time to discount, return to the supplier, or stop reordering.

Pharma's expiry-warning window is configurable, so you can set it to match how your pharmacy actually sells, rather than one fixed number for everything.

What to do with stock that's about to expire

  • Check if your supplier takes returns. Many suppliers will credit or replace stock that's close to its expiry date if you flag it early enough — which is only possible if you know early enough.
  • Move it to the front, physically and digitally. Make sure staff are selling from the oldest batch, and that whatever system you use actually reflects that.
  • Stop reordering it blindly. If a drug keeps expiring unsold, the problem may be your reorder quantity, not your sales staff.

In short: track batch and expiry, not just quantity; set your warning window to match how fast each drug actually sells; and use early warnings to act, not just to know.
